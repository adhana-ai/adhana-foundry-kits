The promises in a subscription contract — and the ones the paperwork leaves open

One contract pack, one model call, a worksheet row per ordered line — plus a review flag taken afterwards, in pure code, from three of the extracted values. Each row carries two calls made by the shipped rulebook: is the line distinct, bundled, or something the contract does not determine; and is it delivered over time, at a point in time, or again not determined. A rate card, a line struck by an amendment and an item continuing under an earlier order form are all in the pack, all carry a line code, and none of them is a promise.

This is a reviewer’s worksheet. It is never an accounting conclusion. It does not determine performance obligations, does not allocate a transaction price, does not conclude on timing, does not open a revenue schedule and does not write a journal entry. A controller does all of that, on the whole arrangement, against the framework their company reports under. The rulebook shipped with this kit is illustrative and is not an authority — it was written for this kit and reproduces no accounting standard, no standard-setter’s guidance, no audit manual and no company’s revenue policy. Replace it with your own before you decide anything real by it.

The review flag is a business condition, not a check on the model: needs_drafting_review fires when the pack carries at least one line the order form prices — a fee of its own — whose separation and pattern the paperwork settles neither of. An open call on an unpriced line is drafting untidiness; the same open call with money attached is a number somebody will have to place, and the contract says neither where it goes nor when. This is the kit’s own simplification, not any company’s review policy — no revenue policy, audit manual, accounting standard or standard-setter’s guidance was consulted, and none is reproduced.
