# Where this corpus came from

**It was written here.** `tools/build_corpus.py` generates every supplier agreement and every
draft order from a fixed seed (`SEED = 20260817`), so the corpus is byte-identical on every
rebuild and there is no third-party data in this kit at all.

| | |
|---|---|
| source | generated by `tools/build_corpus.py`, seed `20260817` |
| licence | **MIT** — this repository's own licence |
| suppliers | 12, in `data/suppliers.json` and `data/agreements/<id>.txt` |
| orders | 72 in `data/orders.jsonl` |
| gold | 72 rows, 5 checks each, in `data/gold.jsonl` — 360 labelled verdicts |
| formats | txt (agreements), jsonl (orders, gold) |

## Why an invented supplier agreement, when real ones exist

**Because a real one cannot be published at all.** A real supplier agreement names a real
counterparty, states real contracted pricing, and carries terms the businesses on both sides of it
would not let leave the building — this repo has never held one and is not able to. There is no
public corpus of paired (draft order, governing agreement) records with adjudicated defects for
the same reason no public corpus of production alerts exists (see `ops-triage`'s own `SOURCES.md`
for that argument in full): the interesting cases are exactly the ones nobody can publish.

**Nothing in here refers to a real company.** Supplier names, SKUs and clause language are
invented; no agreement here states a real contract term.

## Why the agreements are prose, and why the prose varies

Each clause a check needs — contracted cost, minimum order quantity, lead time, approved
ship-to regions, the value band — is written in one of two or three hand-authored phrasings, and
the order the clauses appear in is shuffled per supplier (`agreement_text()` in
`tools/build_corpus.py`). A single fixed template would make this kit a lookup: extracting a
number from "Minimum order quantity is 240 units" is not a different problem from extracting it
from "No line may be ordered below a floor of 240 units," but a checker that only knows the first
sentence cannot tell.

`evals/baseline.py` is a regex-based checker written against the first phrasing it happened to
read, exactly the way a person free-texting a quick script would write one — and it is the honest
floor for exactly that reason. **Measured, not asserted:** it scores **65% overall**, ranging from
100% on `cost` (a loose enough pattern to transfer) down to 25% on `min_qty` (a pattern that only
matches one of two phrasings). See `results/eval-b000-regex.json`.

## The three verdicts, planted and counted

Every order carries a KNOWN verdict per check (`build_orders()` round-robins one planted defect
per order across the five checks, plus one fully-clean order per supplier), so per-check accuracy
is measurable rather than guessed:

| check | clean | defect | unverifiable |
|---|---|---|---|
| cost | 60 | 12 | — |
| min_qty | 60 | 12 | — |
| lead_time | 60 | 12 | — |
| ship_to | 40 | 8 | 24 |
| order_value | 30 | 6 | 36 |

`unverifiable` is not a coin flip — it is forced by an omission the corpus plants on purpose: one
in six suppliers states no approved ship-to list, and one in four states no value-authorization
band. Every order from a supplier missing that field is `unverifiable` on that check, whatever the
order itself says, because there is genuinely nothing to compare against.

## What it cannot tell you

⚠︎ **An invented corpus contains the defects we thought to plant and no others.** Real agreements
carry ambiguity this one does not attempt — a clause that contradicts itself, a superseded
amendment, a hand-written margin note. A checker that clears this corpus has cleared what this
corpus tests, not every way a real agreement can be unclear.

⚠︎ **Every order has exactly one line item family per defect slot.** A real draft order can carry
many lines with independent, overlapping defects; this corpus plants at most one defect type per
order to keep per-check accuracy legible. `breaks_on` on the published report says this plainly.
