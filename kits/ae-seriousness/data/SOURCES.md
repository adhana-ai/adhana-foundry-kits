# Where this corpus came from

**It was written here.** `tools/build_corpus.py` generates every case report from a fixed seed
(`SEED = 20260821`), so the corpus is byte-identical on every rebuild and there is no third-party
data in this kit at all.

| | |
|---|---|
| source | generated by `tools/build_corpus.py`, seed `20260821` |
| licence | **MIT** — this repository's own licence |
| records | 55, in `data/corpus/*.txt` |
| gold | 55 rows in `data/gold.jsonl` |
| drugs / cases | invented INN-style drug names and invented case identifiers, with no link to a real product or a real report |

## Why an invented set of case narratives, when real ones exist

**Because a real adverse-event narrative is among the most identifying text a company holds.** It
describes one person's illness, in their reporter's own words, tied to a named product. There is
no public corpus of (case narrative, adjudicated seriousness) pairs for the same reason there is
no public corpus of bank statements or pay stubs — and publishing one would be worse here.

**So there is no patient and no reporter in this corpus, by construction.** No names, no dates of
birth, no exact ages, no free-text identifiers. Age arrives as a bucketed range and the reporter
arrives as one of four role words. That is not a redaction step that ran afterwards; it is the
shape of the record, and it is what `patient_age_range` is doing in the field schema.

**Nothing in here refers to a real product.** Every drug name is invented in the style of an
international non-proprietary name and is deliberately not a real trade name or a real generic
stem in current use — a synthetic corpus that names a real product implies a real product's
safety record.

## The mechanic: one call per case, ten fields

Each record gives the model a case header — identifier, age band, suspect drug, the event as
described, whether the patient was hospitalised, the outcome, the reporter's own causality
assessment and the reporter's role — plus the free-text case narrative. The model extracts all ten
fields, including whether the case meets the regulatory definition of serious. See `src/prompt.py`.

## The planted ambiguity: "severe" is not "serious"

**This is the distinction the whole kit is built on, and it is a real one.** Across
pharmacovigilance reporting, *serious* is a defined regulatory classification: the case is serious
when the event resulted in death, was life-threatening, required hospitalisation or prolonged an
existing one, produced a persistent or significant disability or incapacity, produced a congenital
anomaly or birth defect, or was medically important enough to require an intervention to prevent
one of those outcomes. *Severe* is a colloquial description of how bad the event felt. **A severe
headache that fully resolved at home is not serious. A mild rash that led to a three-day admission
is.**

`AMBIGUOUS_FRACTION = 0.40` of this corpus's records are written against type on purpose, and
**25 of the 55 landed there**:

- a **serious** case whose report reaches for a mild-register word — a "mild" or "moderate" event
  whose narrative records a hospitalisation, a death, a life-threatening episode, a persistent
  disability, a congenital anomaly, or an intervention given to prevent one of those;
- a **not serious** case whose report reaches for "severe" — an alarming-sounding event that
  resolved at home with no medical attention, no admission and no lasting effect.

A further **7 records use no severity word at all**, so `narrative_severity_word` is legitimately
null there and returning null is the correct answer.

Gold always records the TRUE classification, derived from which generator branch produced the
narrative — never re-derived from a scorer's or a model's own reading of the narrative's surface
tone.

## Two thirds of the serious cases cannot be found by reading a field

Hospitalisation and death are the only two seriousness criteria this record layout states in a
structured field of its own (`hospitalization`, `event_outcome`). The other four —
life-threatening, persistent disability, congenital anomaly, and an event medically important
enough to need an intervention to prevent one of the others — appear **only in the narrative
prose**, with `hospitalization: no` and a non-fatal outcome sitting right above them.

The six criteria are sampled **uniformly**, which is a coverage choice and not a prevalence claim:

| criterion | records | stated in a structured field? |
|---|---|---|
| congenital anomaly | 7 | no — narrative only |
| other medically important | 7 | no — narrative only |
| life-threatening | 6 | no — narrative only |
| hospitalisation | 4 | **yes** |
| death | 4 | **yes** (`event_outcome: fatal`) |
| persistent disability | 3 | no — narrative only |
| none met (not serious) | 24 | — |

⚠︎ **A real safety database is dominated by hospitalisation.** Sampling that way here would leave
a criterion represented by one or two records, where a model missing it entirely would be
invisible in the score — which is exactly what an earlier weighting did, landing congenital
anomaly on a single record. Nothing here is a claim about how often these outcomes occur.

## The keyword floor, and why 0 of 25 is not a coincidence

`evals/baseline.py` answers one question — does the report call the event "severe"? — and returns
that as the regulatory classification. It scores **55 of 55 on every one of the nine structured
fields** (a fixed record layout is mostly regex work) and **27 of 55 on `is_serious`**: 23 of 23
right in the matching register, **0 of 25 in the confusable register**.

The zero is by construction and it is stated rather than hidden: the confusable slice is *defined*
as the records where this floor's one signal is inverted. That is the point of building it — the
gap between the floor and a model that reads the narrative for which criterion was actually met is
the only thing this kit is measuring, and a floor that failed for some other reason would not
measure it.

## The routing rule is this kit's own, not a real program's

`src/extract.py::compute()` fires `needs_review` when a case is BOTH classified `is_serious: yes`
AND the reporter's own `causality_assessment` is `related` or `possibly-related` —
**`CAUSALITY_TRIGGERS`, this kit's own stated simplification.** 15 of this corpus's 55 records
should be flagged.

⚠︎ **This is not a regulatory reporting deadline and it does not model one.** A real expedited
reporting obligation is a *clock*: it starts on a defined awareness event and runs a defined
number of calendar days, and which cases start it depends on the product, the market and the
report type. None of that is here. All this kit computes is "route this one to a human sooner."

⚠︎ **Seriousness and causality are different judgments and the kit keeps them apart.** A case can
be serious and unrelated at the same time — a hospitalised patient who was hit by a car. Those
cases are still cases; they simply do not get the same urgency from this rule. 16 of this corpus's
31 serious records are exactly that, and they are true negatives for the flag rather than misses.

## What it cannot tell you

⚠︎ **This kit classifies and routes only — it never files a report and never starts a clock.**
Every case it flags is routed to a person.

⚠︎ **An invented corpus contains the ambiguity we thought to plant and no others.** A real
pharmacovigilance intake queue carries confusions this one does not attempt — a narrative that
describes an outcome without naming it, a follow-up report that changes the seriousness of a case
already assessed, a narrative in a language the prompt was not written in, a report where the
seriousness criterion is stated by the reporter and contradicted by the attached discharge
summary. A model that clears this corpus has cleared what this corpus tests, not every way a case
narrative can be misread.

⚠︎ **Two allowed values are never exercised.** `hospitalization: unknown` and
`event_outcome: unknown` are in the field schema because a real report can be that incomplete, and
no record here uses either — so nothing measured on this corpus says how the classification
behaves when the deciding fact is simply absent.
