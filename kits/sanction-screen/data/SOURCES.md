# Where this corpus came from

**It was written here.** `tools/build_corpus.py` generates every alert review sheet from a fixed
seed (`SEED = 20260822`), so the corpus is byte-identical on every rebuild and there is no
third-party data in this kit at all.

| | |
|---|---|
| source | generated by `tools/build_corpus.py`, seed `20260822` |
| dataset version | `sanction-screen-2026-08-22-50alerts` |
| licence | **MIT** — this repository's own licence |
| alerts | 50, in `data/corpus/*.txt`, 35,319 bytes |
| gold | 50 rows in `data/gold.jsonl` |
| adjudication rulebook | `data/rulebook.json` — **written for this kit**, see the warning below |
| names / lists / programmes / countries / places / identifiers | **all invented**, see below |

## ⚠︎ The rulebook is illustrative and is NOT an authority

**`data/rulebook.json` was authored for this kit.** It reproduces **no** sanctions programme, **no**
designation or watchlist, **no** supervisor's or regulator's guidance, **no** industry body's
name-matching standard and **no** screening vendor's tuning. Nothing in it was copied from, derived
from, or checked against any such document, because none was consulted.

It is a small, deliberately readable construction whose only job is to make the *decision shape*
measurable: identifiers sorted into strength tiers, a stopping order in which a strong identifier
outranks everything below it, an explicit definition of when a field is even *comparable*, and a
third verdict for the case where the file cannot decide. Several of its simplifications are visibly
not how a real screening desk works, and are named as such in the file itself — most obviously the
absoluteness of check 1, which says a single matching passport number settles an alert outright.

**The real authority for a screening alert is your own sanctions policy, your compliance function
and the people who own the decision.** This kit proposes an adjudication with the deciding
identifier attached and names what it could not determine. **It clears nothing, blocks nothing and
files nothing:** no alert is closed, no party is designated or de-designated, no account is frozen
or released, no payment is stopped or let through, and no report of any kind is made to anybody.
A human makes the call. That sentence is on the kit's own UI, above the answer, and not only here.

## ⚠︎ Every name in this corpus is invented, and none of it refers to anything real

- **People.** Given names and family names are assembled from syllable pools written for this file.
  They are not drawn from any name register, list, directory or dataset. Any resemblance to a
  living person is a coincidence of syllables and not a reference. Three names recur across the
  `insufficient_information` alerts on purpose — that bucket is about ordinary names shared by a lot
  of people — and those three are invented too.
- **Transliteration variants** are produced by a character rule (`v`→`w`, `k`→`kh`, `th`→`t`, and so
  on) applied to an invented name. They are the *shape* a name takes crossing a script, not a
  transliteration of anything.
- **Lists and programmes.** `Consolidated Restricted Parties Index`, `Global Designated Entities
  Register`, `Cross-Border Restricted Persons Schedule`, `Unified Prohibited Counterparties List`;
  `Programme Ashgrove`, `Programme Tallowmere`, `Programme Northline`, `Programme Cindermark`,
  `Programme Quillstone`. **No real list, programme, authority, regime or jurisdiction is named,
  referenced or alluded to anywhere in this kit.**
- **Countries and places.** `Verdania`, `Kastelia`, `Northmarch`, `Oradon`, `Sulmara`, `Tavrinia`,
  `Ellisar`, `Corvant`, `Meridonia`, `Brackenholt`; `Port Ellisar`, `Kadrun`, `Veldt Harbour`,
  `Fennmarch`, `Old Saltrun`, `Highspire`, `Corvant City`, `Lower Thessin`, `Braymoor`,
  `Ilvane Reach`. All invented. No country-specific regime exists in this corpus because no country
  in it exists.
- **Identifiers.** Passport, national-identity and tax-reference numbers are generated from made-up
  formats (`XX0000000`, `NID-0000-0000`, `TRF-00000000`) by the seeded RNG. They are not valid in
  any scheme and check-digit against nothing.
- **Institutions.** None is named. There is no bank, no vendor, no regulator and no country's
  supervisor anywhere in the corpus, the code or the prompts.

## Why an invented set of alerts, when real ones exist

**Because a real screening alert is not something this repo could publish under any circumstances.**
It carries a named living person's date of birth, address, nationality and passport number, an
allegation about them, and an institution's own view of whether it is true. There is no public
corpus of (alert, correct adjudication) pairs and there never will be, for reasons that are much
stronger than the ones that keep bank statements off the internet.

Generating it also makes the label mechanical: gold's `verdict` and `deciding_identifier` are the
rulebook's own lookup run over the same eight values the sheet states, never somebody's reading of
how alike two records look.

## The mechanic: one call per alert, seventeen fields

Each sheet states an alert reference; the list and programme the entry sits on with the screening
engine's own match score; a customer record (name, date of birth, place of birth, nationality, and
one strong identifier or an explicit "no secondary identifier on file"); the watchlist entry beside
it (primary listed name, aliases, and the same four facts); whether the customer's account is
already live; and the triaging analyst's free-text note. The model extracts seventeen fields —
everything except the `Screening List` section, which no field maps to and which is therefore
**never sent** — including the two it has to decide: the adjudication, and the identifier that
produced it. See `src/prompt.py`.

**The rulebook is sent with every call.** "Which identifier outranks which" is a policy, and a
model cannot apply a policy it has never been shown. `src/prompt.py::rulebook_block()` renders
`data/rulebook.json` into the prompt rather than restating it in prose, so the instructions and the
gold labels cannot drift apart about the same policy. Measured on the worked example: 973 of 3,105
input tokens (31%) are the rulebook, and 2,850 of 3,105 (92%) is a fixed prefix paid on every call.

## The rule, and the stopping order a reader gets wrong

```
adjudicate(customer_id_type, customer_id_value, listed_id_type, listed_id_value,
           customer_dob, listed_dob, customer_pob, listed_pob):
    a strong identifier of the SAME TYPE on both, values equal   -> same_party
    a strong identifier of the SAME TYPE on both, values differ  -> not_a_match
    any comparable moderate identifier that DISAGREES            -> not_a_match
    at least 2 comparable moderate identifiers that AGREE         -> same_party
    otherwise                                                     -> insufficient_information
```

Five things about that rule are stated to the model in full, because each is a reading a careless
reader falls into on its own — and each is measured on this corpus rather than asserted:

| | | measured here |
|---|---|---|
| **a strong identifier outranks every weak disagreement** | different spelling, different nationality, **different place of birth**, low engine score — and the same passport number. Same party. Hide the identifier and the sheet reads `not_a_match`, asserted directly in `evals/check_labels.py` | **6 alerts** |
| **and it outranks every weak agreement too** | identical name, identical full date of birth, identical place of birth, identical nationality, engine score 0.9+ — and two different passport numbers. Not a match. Hide the identifier and the sheet reads `same_party` | **8 alerts** |
| **two identifiers of DIFFERENT types corroborate nothing** | a passport number against a national identity number is two facts about two registries. Not weak evidence — no evidence, and the rule falls through as though neither record carried one | **8 alerts** |
| **a partial date of birth is a stated fact and still not comparable** | `1978` against `1978-04-12` neither agrees nor disagrees. Complete it and the same sheet reads `same_party` | **7 alerts** |
| **a common name with nothing else is the answer, not a gap in it** | no comparable identifier, no comparable date, no place published. `insufficient_information`, with a named reason and what would settle it | **8 alerts** |

The rule lives in one function — `src/rulebook.py::decide()` — used to write gold, stated to the
model by `src/prompt.py`, and re-run over the model's own reply by `evals/judge.py`. Three readers,
one definition, so the corpus, the prompt and the diagnostic cannot drift about what a verdict
means.

## The planted ambiguity: the note follows the engine's score, and the score is decorrelated

The analyst's note is written by somebody who saw two names and a similarity number and had **not
yet compared identifiers**, so its register follows the engine's match score and nothing else:

- score ≥ 0.85 → a **confident** note ("I am satisfied these are the same party");
- 0.70–0.84 → a **hedging** note ("Honestly cannot separate these two from what is on the file");
- below 0.70 → a **dismissive** note ("Different person, clearly. Nothing beyond the name lines up").

**The contradiction rate is measured, not dialled in.** Because name similarity is decorrelated from
identity here — for the same reason screening false positives exist — the note's register
contradicts the rulebook's verdict on **35 of 50 alerts (70%)**. That number is an emergent property
of the construction, printed by `tools/build_corpus.py` and floored in `evals/check_labels.py`
rather than set as a target. Gold is **never** derived from the note.

`evals/check_labels.py` re-runs the rulebook lookup over every gold row before a run is allowed to
spend, and fails if a single label disagrees with its own values — and separately asserts that every
note template classifies to the register it was written as, against the free floor's own keyword
list, before anything may spend. This corpus contains that trap on purpose: a **confident** note
reads *"I am satisfied these are the same party"*, and the dismissive phrase the floor looks for is
the whole negated form *"not the same party"*. A single-word keyword list would have fired on it — a
sibling kit earlier in this series shipped exactly that defect and mis-registered four records for
days before it was caught.

## The composition is exact, then shuffled — not drawn per alert

Each of the seven buckets is a fixed **count**, shuffled by the seeded RNG, and every constructor is
asserted against the rulebook at build time — a constructor that quietly stops producing its own
bucket is exactly the defect an exact composition exists to prevent.

| | |
|---|---|
| `same_party` | 20 |
| — a shared strong identifier, everything else ordinary | 8 |
| — a shared strong identifier under a transliterated name, with the nationality **and** the place of birth disagreeing | 6 |
| — no comparable strong identifier; full date **and** place of birth both agree | 6 |
| `not_a_match` | 15 |
| — a strong identifier of the same type with a different value, everything else agreeing | 8 |
| — no comparable strong identifier; one comparable moderate identifier conflicts (4 on the date, 3 on the place) | 7 |
| `insufficient_information` | 15 |
| — a common name with nothing comparable at all | 8 |
| — a partial date of birth on the watchlist entry, place of birth agreeing | 7 |
| two strong identifiers of **different types** side by side | 8 |
| analyst note whose register contradicts the verdict | 35 (70%) |
| account already live | 24 |
| not dismissible **and** already live — the escalation flag | 17 |

`deciding_identifier` is not degenerate either: 22 alerts are decided by a strong identifier
(9 national identity number, 7 passport number, 6 tax reference), 6 by the date and place of birth
together, 4 by the date alone, 3 by the place alone, and 15 by nothing on the file.

## The guardrail is a business condition, not a check on the model

`src/extract.py::compute()` raises `needs_escalation` when the verdict is anything other than
`not_a_match` **and** the customer's account is already `live`. A pending onboarding can wait for a
person to work the alert; the same verdict on a live account means money is moving for a party
nobody has adjudicated. `insufficient_information` counts here too, deliberately and emphatically —
an alert nobody can decide, on an account that is already running, is precisely the one to put in
front of a human, and treating "cannot tell" as a clearance is the single worst thing anything in
this kit could do.

⚠︎ **It orders a worklist and nothing else.** It does not freeze the account, stop a payment, close
or escalate a case, or notify anybody. And it is **this kit's own simplification, not anybody's real
alert-handling policy** — no supervisor's guidance, institution's procedure or regulatory rule was
consulted, and none is reproduced. A real desk weighs the exposure, the customer's own risk rating,
how long the alert has been open and who is allowed to decide it.

## What it cannot tell you

⚠︎ **This kit adjudicates on paper only.** `verdict` and `deciding_identifier` are extracted fields
and `needs_escalation` is a routing signal; nothing here disposes of anything, and nothing in the
kit performs any write or any outbound call other than the single completion request per alert.

⚠︎ **A run that scores perfectly has agreed with `data/rulebook.json`, which is a much smaller claim
than being right about a real alert.** The graders measure whether the run applied the shipped
rulebook. They cannot measure whether the rulebook is correct, because the rulebook is this kit's
own construction and nobody in a compliance function has looked at it.

⚠︎ **An invented corpus contains the ambiguity we thought to plant and no others.** A real alert
queue carries confusions this one does not attempt — a legal entity rather than a natural person, a
second passport under a former name, a genuine name change, an address history, an ownership chain,
a relative with the same name and a near date of birth, a list entry whose own identifiers are
wrong, an identifier transcribed with a transposed digit, and a customer record whose data quality
is the real problem. A model that clears this corpus has cleared what this corpus tests, not every
way an alert adjudication can go wrong.

⚠︎ **And it is not a screening system.** It runs no matching engine, subscribes to no list, and
knows nothing about why an entry was published. It reads the sheet in front of it.
