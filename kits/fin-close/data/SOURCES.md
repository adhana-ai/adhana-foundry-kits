# Where this corpus came from

**It was written here.** `tools/build_corpus.py` generates every recurring-JE basis document and
every close cycle from a fixed seed (`SEED = 20260819`), so the corpus is byte-identical on every
rebuild and there is no third-party data in this kit at all.

| | |
|---|---|
| source | generated by `tools/build_corpus.py`, seed `20260819` |
| licence | **MIT** — this repository's own licence |
| recurring-JE templates | 12, in `data/templates.json` and `data/basis/<id>.txt` |
| close cycles | 60 in `data/close_cycles.jsonl` |
| gold | 60 rows, 4 checks each, in `data/gold.jsonl` — 240 labelled verdicts |
| formats | txt (basis documents, 12 files), jsonl (close cycles, 1 file) — 30,785 bytes measured, `gold.jsonl` excluded (it is the labelled eval set, not the corpus a forker points their own data at) |

## Why an invented close package, when real ones exist

**Because a real one cannot be published at all.** A real month-end close package names real GL
accounts, real contracted amounts (an actual lease, an actual depreciation schedule) and carries
figures a finance team would not let leave the building — this repo has never held one and is not
able to. There is no public corpus of paired (draft JE, prior-period approved counterpart,
reconciliation worksheet) records with adjudicated defects, for the same reason no public corpus of
production alerts or supplier agreements exists: the interesting cases are exactly the ones nobody
can publish.

**Nothing in here refers to a real company.** Account names, entry amounts, preparer and reviewer
names, and basis narratives are invented; no basis document here states a real contracted figure.

## Why the basis documents are prose, and why the prose varies

Each fact a check needs — the posting account, the prior-period approved amount, the calculation
basis, the reconciliation materiality band — is written in one of two or three hand-authored
phrasings, and the order the paragraphs appear in is shuffled per template (`basis_text()` in
`tools/build_corpus.py`). A single fixed template would make this kit a lookup: extracting an amount
from "The prior-period approved amount for this recurring entry was $28,563.79" is not a different
problem from extracting it from "Last period's approved posting under this recurring template
totaled $28,563.79," but a checker that only knows the first sentence cannot tell.

`evals/baseline.py` is a regex-based checker written against the first phrasing it happened to read,
exactly the way a person free-texting a quick script would write one — and it is the honest floor
for exactly that reason. See `results/eval-b000-regex.json` for the measured number once
`evals/baseline.py` has run — it is not typed here from intent.

## The three verdicts, planted and counted

Every close cycle carries a KNOWN verdict per check (`build_cycles()` round-robins one planted
defect per cycle across the four checks, plus one fully-clean cycle per template), so per-check
accuracy is measurable rather than guessed:

| check | clean | defect | unverifiable |
|---|---|---|---|
| account | 48 | 12 | — |
| amount | 48 | 12 | — |
| basis | 40 | 10 | 10 |
| residual | 36 | 9 | 15 |

`unverifiable` is not a coin flip — it is forced by an omission the corpus plants on purpose, by a
fixed sample rather than an independent draw per template (see `NO_BASIS_N` / `NO_THRESHOLD_N` in
`tools/build_corpus.py` — an independent per-template coin flip can legitimately land on zero
omissions across 12 templates, which would silently ship a kit whose flagship unverifiable case
never appears): exactly 2 of the 12 recurring-JE templates (one in six) state no calculation basis
at all — "the calculation method for this recurring entry is not recorded in this workpaper" — and
exactly 3 of the 12 (one in four) state no reconciliation materiality band for their account. Every
close cycle under a basis-less template is `unverifiable` on the basis check, and every close cycle
under a threshold-less account is `unverifiable` on the residual check, whatever the drafted entry
or the reconciliation numbers say — because there is genuinely nothing to compare against.

This is the corpus expression of the flagged open question: **this kit assumes each recurring JE's
documented basis is stored somewhere machine-readable, rather than held only in a preparer's memory
or a prior-year workpaper.** The 2 basis-less templates are the corpus's way of making that
assumption fail on purpose, so the eval can measure what happens when it does — the honest answer
being `unverifiable`, never a guessed `clean`.

## What it cannot tell you

⚠︎ **An invented corpus contains the defects we thought to plant and no others.** A real close
package carries ambiguity this one does not attempt — an entry restated mid-quarter, a basis change
that was documented in an email nobody attached to the workpaper, a reconciling item that is a
genuine judgment call rather than a threshold comparison. A checker that clears this corpus has
cleared what this corpus tests, not every way a real close can go wrong.

⚠︎ **Every close cycle carries at most one planted defect.** A real month-end close can carry
several independent problems on the same entry at once — a changed basis AND a stale account, say;
this corpus plants at most one defect type per cycle to keep per-check accuracy legible. `breaks_on`
on the published report says this plainly.

⚠︎ **The residual check is a threshold comparison, not a judgment call.** A real reviewer sometimes
accepts an above-threshold residual with a documented explanation (a known timing difference, an
in-transit item) and sometimes flags a below-threshold one anyway because something else looks
wrong. This corpus's gold residual verdict is derived mechanically from the materiality band alone —
it measures whether the model respects the documented threshold, not whether it can reproduce every
judgment call a human reviewer might make on top of it.
