# Where this corpus came from

**It was written here.** `tools/build_corpus.py` generates every records-disposition review from a
fixed seed (`SEED = 20260822`), so the corpus is byte-identical on every rebuild and there is no
third-party data in this kit at all.

| | |
|---|---|
| source | generated by `tools/build_corpus.py`, seed `20260822` |
| dataset version | `hold-conflict-2026-08-22-55reviews` |
| licence | **MIT** — this repository's own licence |
| records | 55, in `data/corpus/*.txt`, 40,925 bytes |
| gold | 55 rows in `data/gold.jsonl` |
| series / offices / projects / holds / people | invented identifiers, invented custodian offices, invented project names, invented hold ids, and **no person named anywhere** — with no link to a real agency, a real published retention schedule or a real matter |
| review date | **2026-08**, one constant for the whole corpus |

## Why an invented set of disposition reviews, when real ones exist

**Because a real records-disposition review is not something this repo can publish either.** It
names a real agency's series, a real custodian, a real litigation matter or public-records
requester, and a records officer's own remark about a specific file. There is no public corpus of
(disposition review, correct eligibility) pairs, for the same reason there is no public corpus of
bank statements or personnel files.

**No real agency, real filed general records schedule, real disposition authority, real litigation
matter and no real person is named or reproduced anywhere in this kit.** "Record series", "record
category", "retention schedule", "cutoff", "legal hold" and "disposition" are ordinary
public-sector records-management vocabulary, and the SHAPE of the schedule table here — an item
code, a series description, a retention period in years — is modelled on how jurisdictions publish
general schedules. Every code, every period, every series, every office, every project and every
hold notice was invented for this corpus.

## What this kit decides, and what it never does

⚠︎ **It proposes; a records officer releases.** `disposition_eligible` answers "may this series be
PUT IN FRONT OF a person for destruction approval". Nothing in this kit destroys, deletes or
disposes of anything, there is no write path in the app, and the guardrail downstream is the
opposite of a disposal action — it asks for a series to be pulled back **out** of a destruction
queue.

## The mechanic: one call per review, twelve fields

Each review states a record series, the office that holds it, its title, its records category, the
project it relates to, the month it closed, its general-schedule item and retention period, when
that retention expires, any longer-retention overlapping series, the hold registry, whether the
series is already in the destruction queue, and the records officer's own free-text note. The
model extracts twelve fields — everything except the custodian office, which no field maps to and
which is therefore never sent — including the two it has to decide: which hold (if any) freezes
the series, and whether it may be proposed for disposition. See `src/prompt.py`.

## The rule, and the priority order a reader gets wrong

```
disposition_eligible(binding_hold, overlapping_expires, retention_expires), as of 2026-08:
    a hold binds it                                   -> no
    an overlapping series has NOT expired              -> no
    its own retention has NOT expired                  -> no
    otherwise                                          -> yes

binding_hold: the FIRST registry line that is `active` AND whose scope covers this series.
              A line whose scope reads "continues the scope of <id>" takes that hold's scope.

scope covers  <=>  category is one the scope names
              AND  the scope names this project, or any project
              AND  the year in record_closed is inside the scope's span
```

**All three coverage tests must pass.** Two of three passing is the commonest wrong answer on this
corpus, and it is the one that looks most like a match.

## The four hard cases, each planted in quantity

A corpus that plants one instance of its own sharpest test has an anecdote, not a measurement.
`evals/check_labels.py` refuses to let a run spend if any of these falls below five.

| class | n | what it tests |
|---|---|---|
| `hold_active` | 10 | an active hold whose scope covers outright — the base case |
| `hold_successor` | 6 | **a released hold with a live successor.** The covering hold reads `released`; a second, ACTIVE registry line's scope is only "continues the scope of `<id>`". Reading the released line and stopping answers "eligible", and is wrong |
| `released_no_successor` | 5 | **the mirror**, and it is what stops "released means look for a successor" from being a working shortcut: a released hold that WOULD have covered, with nothing following it. Eligible |
| `scope_category_miss` | 6 | **partial coverage.** Right project, right dates, the hold is about a different records category. Eligible |
| `scope_project_miss` | 5 | **partial coverage.** Right category, right dates, the hold names a different project. Eligible |
| `scope_date_miss` | 6 | **the date range excludes the record.** Right category, right project, and the series closed outside the scope's span. Eligible |
| `overlap_longer` | 6 | **eligible under its own schedule, captured by a longer one.** No hold binds, its own retention has run out, and a longer-retention overlapping series has not. Frozen |
| `retention_open` | 6 | its own retention period has simply not elapsed yet. Frozen |
| `clear` | 5 | nothing on file at all. Eligible |

Twenty of the 55 reviews carry an overlapping series; on 14 of them it has already expired and
decides nothing, so its presence is not a giveaway. Forty of the 55 carry at least one hold on
file, and only **16** of those holds actually bind — which is the whole reason the over-cautious
floor below scores what it does.

## The planted ambiguity: eligibility is structured, and the note disagrees with it

`N_AMBIGUOUS = 22` puts a records-officer note from the wrong register on exactly 40% of records:

- a genuinely frozen series carrying a breezy note — "Routine end-of-retention item. Nothing
  outstanding on this series.";
- a genuinely eligible series carrying a note that reads like a problem — "Escalated last cycle
  over a possible hold conflict -- pending supervisor review."

Gold's `disposition_eligible` is **never** derived from the note. It is the three-condition rule,
run over the same values the record itself states.

`evals/check_labels.py` re-runs that derivation over every gold row before a run is allowed to
spend, and fails if any label disagrees with its own values; separately it asserts that every
class produced the verdict it is named for, that the successor case is findable only by following
the reference, that the mirror case carries no successor at all, and that every note template
classifies to the register it was written as — before anything may spend.

## The composition is exact, then shuffled — not drawn per record

Each class is a fixed **count**, shuffled by the seeded RNG, rather than drawn from an independent
coin per record. The corpus is still deterministic and still byte-identical on every run; what
changes is that the numbers on the page are the numbers the design asked for.

The mix on this seed:

| | |
|---|---|
| eligible for disposition | 27 |
| frozen | 28 |
| — frozen by a hold | 16 |
| — frozen by a live overlapping series | 6 |
| — frozen because its own retention has not elapsed | 6 |
| carrying at least one hold on file | 40 |
| carrying an overlapping series (expired or not) | 20 |
| officer note from the contradicting register | 22 (40%) |
| already in the destruction queue | 30 |
| frozen **and** already queued — the review flag | 16 |

## Two free floors, and they fail in opposite directions

Sibling kits in this series ship one free baseline. This corpus offers two shortcuts, and the pair
is the finding:

| floor | what it does | eligibility accuracy | the direction it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| `b000-holds` | **the over-cautious clerk** — any hold on file means "not eligible" | **41.82%** | 22 series frozen that nothing actually holds, **and 10 frozen series released** — it is blind to both ways a series is frozen with no hold on it |
| `b001-notes` | **the tone reader** — decides from how the officer's note reads | **60.00%** | 13 frozen series released, 9 eligible ones frozen — exactly the 22 records the corpus plants a contradicting note on, plus the ones where tone happens to agree |

Both floors read `queue_status` correctly by regex every time and still fail the review flag,
because a business-condition guardrail is only ever as good as the field it reads.

⚠︎ It would be trivial to make the `holds` floor perfect on this corpus and that is the point of
not doing it: the registry lines here are machine-written in a fixed four-column format, so a
regex could parse the scope prose back into its pieces and re-run the coverage test exactly. It
would score 100% here and tell you nothing, because a real hold registry is prose in a
case-management system with no fixed line format at all.

## The guardrail is a business condition, not a check on the model

`src/extract.py::compute()` routes a series for a records-officer follow-up when it is **not**
eligible **and** it is already sitting in the destruction queue. A frozen series nobody has queued
can be left where it is until the next cycle; the same series already in the queue is one approval
away from being destroyed under a live hold.

⚠︎ **Note the direction — this flag never proposes a destruction. It asks for one to be stopped.**

⚠︎ **This is the kit's own simplification, not a real records programme's escalation policy.** No
published general records schedule, disposition authority or hold procedure was consulted, and
none is reproduced. A real records office weighs how close the destruction batch is, who issued
the hold, whether counsel has been notified, and whether the series has already been certified for
destruction; this is two values, chosen because it is the smallest rule that is genuinely useful
and readable off one reply.

## What it cannot tell you

⚠︎ **This kit proposes and routes only — it never destroys a record, releases a hold, amends a
retention schedule or notifies anybody.** `disposition_eligible` is an extracted field and
`needs_review` is a routing signal; nothing here disposes of anything.

⚠︎ **A hold scope here is one sentence generated from four structured pieces.** A real hold notice
is drafted by counsel, runs to paragraphs, and describes its scope in language that was negotiated
rather than templated — "all documents concerning the design, permitting or construction of the
Riverside corridor, including but not limited to...". A model that reads this corpus's scopes
correctly has read a sentence with a category list, a project and a date span in it; that is the
shape of the judgement, not its full difficulty.

⚠︎ **An invented corpus contains the ambiguity we thought to plant and no others.** A real
disposition queue carries confusions this one does not attempt — a hold whose scope is described
by custodian rather than by series, a series that was reclassified mid-retention, a partial
release covering some custodians and not others, a schedule item superseded by a newer general
schedule, or a record that exists in two formats under two different items. A model that clears
this corpus has cleared what this corpus tests, not every way a disposition decision can go wrong.
