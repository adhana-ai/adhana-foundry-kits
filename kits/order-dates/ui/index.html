Every deadline a scheduling order sets, and the day it lands on

One scheduling order, one model call, a row per obligation — plus the calendar date recomputed afterwards in pure code from the shipped counting rules. The arithmetic is the hard part, not the reading: whether the day the clock starts on is counted, whether the period is in business days or calendar days, and what happens when it lands on a weekend or a court holiday. A party’s own calculation, where the order carries one, is copied as a field and decides nothing.

This computes a proposed calendar. It does not file, serve, docket or waive anything. Nothing here is legal advice, nothing is a substitute for the file and the rules that actually govern it, and no date on this page goes anywhere until a person puts it there. The counting rulebook shipped with this kit is invented and is not an authority — Meridian Vale is not a place, MV-CR-1 is not anybody’s procedure, and every court holiday on its list was made up for this kit. Replace it with your own before you calendar anything real by it.

The Recomputed column is src/calendar_rules.py::compute() re-run over the model’s own extracted values — the same function that wrote this kit’s gold and the same one stated to the model in words. Where the two columns disagree, the reply contradicts the rulebook applied to what the reply itself read, and that is visible without any labels at all. A row whose recomputed date is cannot be dated is one the Order does not date: its period runs from an event the Recorded Events table leaves blank. That is a real answer, not a failure to produce one — and it is not the Order date, not zero days and not a guess. A confident wrong date on a docketing queue is worse than a blank, because a blank gets chased and a date gets diarised.
