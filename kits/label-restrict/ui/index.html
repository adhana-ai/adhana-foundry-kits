Read the restrictions off a label, and check one proposed application against them

One label extract and one proposed application, one model call, a field table — plus the decision taken afterwards, in pure code, by walking eight restriction checks in a fixed order and stopping at the first that fires. The answer is two things, not one: may this application be made as proposed, and which restriction settled it. A right verdict attributed to the wrong restriction is a wrong answer here, because a grower told to wait the wrong number of the wrong unit from the wrong date has not been helped. The agronomist’s note and the previous season’s application count are both on the page, and neither decides anything.

This proposes a reading of a label. It never authorises an application. A qualified adviser decides, against the approved label for this product in this territory and the conditions of its registration. The check set shipped with this kit is illustrative and is not an authority — it was written for this kit and reproduces no real product label, no manufacturer’s instructions and no regulator’s guidance. Every product name, registration number and active substance in this corpus is invented. Replace all of it with your own before you decide anything real by it.

The hold flag is a business condition, not a check on the model: needs_hold fires when verdict is anything other than within_label and application_status is applied. A planned application can simply be changed before the sprayer goes out; the same verdict on one already made means the product is on the crop, which is the case somebody has to deal with today. insufficient_information counts here too — treating an unknown as a pass is the one thing a label check must never do. This is the kit’s own simplification, not anybody’s real procedure — no manufacturer’s guidance, assurance-scheme rule or regulation was consulted, and none is reproduced.
